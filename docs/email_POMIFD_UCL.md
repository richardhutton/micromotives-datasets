Dear Dr Blumenau,

Your Harvard Dataverse deposit for "The Variable Persuasiveness of Political Rhetoric"
(doi:10.7910/DVN/POMIFD) says "You may wish to communicate with the Contact(s) specified
before use", so I am doing that.

I am building a UK dataset for testing how well language models predict real people's
survey answers, following SocSci210 (arXiv:2509.05830), which did this for 210 US
experiments. Each row gives a model a respondent's demographics and the exact material
that respondent was shown, and scores it on whether it predicts the answer they actually
gave.

Yours is the study I would most like to include, for two reasons.

The first is that your material is real writing. Almost everything else I have is
conjoint, so the stimulus is a list of attribute levels that I have to turn into prose
myself. Your arguments came out of Commons debates and went to respondents as written, so
there is nothing for me to reconstruct and no chance of my wording standing in for yours.

The second is that it is a hard test. Your own finding is that the same kind of argument
can persuade on one issue and fall flat on another. That means a model cannot do well here
by luck — it has to get the answer right across 336 arguments where the right answer keeps
changing. A study built on one argument lets a model look clever on a single lucky guess;
yours does not.

I am not redistributing anything. Nothing derived from your data would be published or
shared with anyone; this is for my own research use, and your article would be cited.

If you would rather I did not use it, or there are parts to leave out, please just say.

## Notes for rich, not for sending

The two reasons, one line each, so you can defend them if asked:

1. **It is already text.** Every conjoint we hold gives us attribute levels and we render
   the prose ourselves — which means the condition is partly OUR writing, and that is the
   one part of a row nothing downstream can check. His arguments went to respondents
   exactly as they sit in the deposit.
2. **It cannot be flukéd.** His paper's result is that persuasiveness is unstable across
   issues. If the right answer swings around unpredictably, a lucky guess cannot survive
   336 of them, so a good score actually means something.
